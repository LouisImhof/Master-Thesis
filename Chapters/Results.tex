\section{Results}

This chapter presents and interprets the out-of-sample results for the three main models and the image encoding comparison experiment across the four research hypotheses. Section~\ref{sec:res_h1} covers H1 (predictive power in European equities). Section~\ref{sec:res_h2} covers H2 (architectural improvements) including the image encoding comparison. Section~\ref{sec:res_h3} discusses H3 (geographical generalisability). Section~\ref{sec:res_h4} covers H4 (cryptocurrency transfer). Section~\ref{sec:portfolio} reports the portfolio performance, and Section~\ref{sec:temporal} covers temporal and regime analysis.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{H1: Predictive Power in European Equities}
\label{sec:res_h1}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Table~\ref{tab:main_metrics} shows the full set of classification metrics for the three main models over the out-of-sample test period 2021--2026.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Out-of-Sample Classification Metrics, Main Models (Test Period 2021--2026)}
\label{tab:main_metrics}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{AUC} & \textbf{95\% CI} & \textbf{Acc} & \textbf{F1 (macro)} & \textbf{IC} \\
\midrule
Baseline CNN   & 0.5166 & [0.512, 0.521] & 0.552 & 0.438 & 0.029 \\
EfficientNet-B2 & 0.5160 & [0.511, 0.520] & 0.558 & 0.437 & 0.028 \\
ConvNeXt V2    & 0.5179 & [0.513, 0.522] & 0.546 & 0.477 & 0.031 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

All three models achieve AUC above 0.5, with 95\% confidence intervals that lie entirely above 0.5. This provides evidence against the null hypothesis $H_{10}$ that no model has any predictive power in European equities. The result holds even for the simplest model, the Baseline CNN, which was designed to replicate an existing published architecture.

One caveat applies to the confidence intervals. The bootstrap resamples individual stock-day observations, but adjacent days in the test set share up to 19 of their 20 input days, and the 50 names move substantially together cross-sectionally. The effective number of independent observations is therefore considerably smaller than the raw count of 66,932, and the reported intervals are likely narrower than a block or date-clustered bootstrap would produce. The point estimates consistently exceed 0.5 across all three models, which is the primary evidence for H1; the confidence intervals should be read as approximations of the uncertainty rather than precise frequentist coverage statements.

The predictive signal is modest. AUC values in the range 0.51--0.52 represent a small but real ability to rank stocks by their likely direction over the next 20 trading days, consistent with the level of signal typically found in academic return prediction studies \parencite{Gu2020}. They do not imply that the models can reliably classify any individual stock as up or down.

The ROC curves and calibration plots for all three models are shown in Figures~\ref{fig:roc_curves} and~\ref{fig:calibration}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Graphics/roc_curves.png}
\caption{ROC curves for the three main models over the out-of-sample test period 2021--2026. All three models lie above the diagonal, confirming positive directional skill.}
\label{fig:roc_curves}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Graphics/calibration.png}
\caption{Calibration curves for the three main models. The Brier Scores are 0.247 (Baseline CNN), 0.248 (EfficientNet-B2), and 0.248 (ConvNeXt V2). All models are reasonably calibrated around the 0.55 class frequency.}
\label{fig:calibration}
\end{figure}

The Brier Scores cluster around 0.25, close to the Brier Score of an uninformative model predicting the base rate of 0.55 throughout. This is expected given the small AUC values and reflects how difficult short-term equity return prediction is. The IC values are around 0.02--0.03, indicating positive but small cross-sectional alignment between the model's rankings and actual outcomes.

\subsection{H2: Architecture Comparison and Image Encoding}

\subsubsection{Main Architecture Comparison}

From Table~\ref{tab:main_metrics}, ConvNeXt V2 achieves the highest AUC at 0.5179, followed by the Baseline CNN at 0.5166 and EfficientNet-B2 at 0.5160. The gap between the best model and the baseline is only 0.001 AUC points. As noted later in this section, single-run training variance from random seed differences and early stopping timing amounts to around 0.004--0.005 AUC points, which exceeds the entire spread across the three models. The ranking in Table~\ref{tab:main_metrics} should therefore not be interpreted as a stable ordering of architectures.

The confusion matrices in Figure~\ref{fig:confusion_matrices} reveal an important qualitative difference between the models. The Baseline CNN and ConvNeXt V2 show a bias toward predicting Up, which is consistent with the positive class frequency of 55\% in the data. EfficientNet-B2, by contrast, produces a more balanced distribution of predictions. The EfficientNet model with Focal Loss and label smoothing was calibrated specifically to avoid the over-prediction of the majority class.

\begin{figure}[H]

\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Graphics/confusion_matrices.png}
\caption{Confusion matrices for the three main models. Baseline CNN and ConvNeXt V2 tend to predict Up more often, while EfficientNet-B2 is more balanced.}
\label{fig:confusion_matrices}
\end{figure}

The prediction score distributions are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pred_distribution}.

\begin{figure}[H]

The advanced models do not significantly outperform the Baseline CNN, so $H_{20}$ (that the advanced models beat the baseline) cannot be rejected at conventional significance levels. The added complexity of the pretrained backbone and the sequence encoder does not translate into a meaningful AUC improvement on this dataset. This is consistent with the broader literature, where improvements from more complex architectures tend to be small in financial prediction tasks \parencite{Gu2020}.

One limitation of this comparison should be noted. The v3 and v4 configurations differ not only in image format but also in the number of technical indicators fed to the sequence encoder: 21 for v3 and 25 for v4. The performance advantage of v4 could therefore partly reflect the four additional indicators rather than the multi-scale image structure alone. A fully controlled ablation, holding the indicator set fixed while varying only the image format, would be needed to cleanly attribute the improvement to the encoding choice. This is left for future work. The conclusion is that the v4 \emph{configuration} outperforms v3, with the image format as the most likely primary driver, but the confound cannot be fully ruled out from the current experiment.

\subsection{H3: Geographical Generalisability}
\label{sec:res_h3}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\textcite{Jiang2023} report AUC values of around 0.54--0.56 for their best models on U.S. equities over the period 1993--2020 under the I20R20 specification. Their headline results are typically presented as Sharpe Ratios from the resulting trading strategy, but they also tabulate AUC-ROC as a classification metric, which is what we compare here. The European models in this thesis achieve AUC values of 0.516--0.518 over the test period 2021--2026. This places European predictability below the U.S. benchmark by around 2--3 AUC percentage points.

Several factors make a direct comparison difficult. The evaluation periods do not overlap: the U.S. results cover 1993--2020 and the European results cover 2021--2026. The post-pandemic period may be genuinely harder to predict, as it involved unprecedented monetary policy shifts and unusual cross-sectional return dynamics. In addition, the Euro Stoxx 50 has only 50 stocks, while \textcite{Jiang2023} work with thousands of U.S. stocks. The smaller cross-section reduces statistical power and limits the model's ability to learn from diverse pattern examples.

With these caveats, the European results are in the same general range as the U.S. results rather than far below them. The signal exists in European equities, but it appears somewhat weaker, which is in line with findings from international studies suggesting that European markets are somewhat more efficient than U.S. markets for momentum-based strategies \parencite{cakici2023}.

With only two assets, a cross-sectional IC per date reduces to a directional hit-rate rather than a meaningful rank correlation, so the IC figures in Table~\ref{tab:crypto_metrics} should be interpreted as directional accuracy measures rather than cross-sectional signal estimates. ConvNeXt V2's higher IC reflects the model predicting the correct relative direction of BTC versus ETH more often than not, though this interpretation is limited by the two-asset constraint. Whether this reflects a genuine transferable signal or an artefact of the specific test period is not fully clear, and the small sample limits the reliability of the finding.

\subsection{Portfolio Performance}
\label{sec:portfolio}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Table~\ref{tab:portfolio} reports the corrected portfolio results for the three main models using actual 20-day forward returns and non-overlapping rebalancing every 20 trading days, as described in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}. The strategy is a cross-sectional quintile long-short: long the top quintile and short the bottom quintile by predicted up-probability, equal-weighted and dollar-neutral, with 10 basis points round-trip cost deducted.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering

\label{tab:portfolio}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccccccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{Gross Ret.} & \textbf{Net Ret.} & \textbf{Sharpe (net)} & \textbf{Sortino (net)} & \textbf{Max DD} & \textbf{NW $t$} & \textbf{$p$} \\
\midrule
Baseline CNN    & $+$5.1\% & $+$3.9\% & 0.32 & 0.54 & $-$0.165 & 0.77 & 0.446 \\
EfficientNet-B2 & $+$0.7\% & $-$0.6\% & $-$0.06 & $-$0.10 & $-$0.161 & $-$0.15 & 0.884 \\
ConvNeXt V2     & $+$5.1\% & $+$3.8\% & 0.36 & 0.62 & $-$0.184 & 0.87 & 0.389 \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\smallskip\par
{\small \textit{Note}: Returns are annualised. All models: $N = 65$ non-overlapping 20-day periods. Newey-West $t$-statistics use 1 lag. Gross/net returns are the annualised mean period return before and after 10 bps round-trip cost. The EW Benchmark is an equal-weighted buy-and-hold across all 50 constituents on the same rebalance dates.}
\end{table}

Baseline CNN and ConvNeXt V2 both produce positive gross annualised returns of around 5\%, with net Sharpe Ratios of 0.32 and 0.36 respectively. EfficientNet-B2 generates a near-zero gross return that turns slightly negative after transaction costs. None of the three models achieves a statistically significant return at conventional levels: the Newey-West $t$-statistics range from $-$0.15 to 0.87, all with $p > 0.05$.

The lack of statistical significance is not a surprise. With a 20-day holding period and a five-year test window, the backtest contains only 65 non-overlapping independent periods. To reach a $t$-statistic above 2.0 with $N = 65$, a net Sharpe Ratio above approximately 0.80 would be required. The signal in these models, while positive for two of the three architectures, is not large enough to reach conventional significance thresholds given the available test sample. This is a fundamental constraint of evaluating a 20-day-horizon strategy over a five-year window and applies regardless of which model is used.

A further caveat concerns the comparison with \textcite{Jiang2023}, whose trading results on U.S. equities show substantially higher Sharpe Ratios. Two structural differences explain the gap. First, their universe contains several thousand stocks rather than 50, so each portfolio leg holds hundreds of positions per rebalance date and stock-level noise diversifies almost entirely away, leaving only the systematic directional signal in the portfolio return. With roughly 10 stocks per leg here, that noise is the dominant component. Second, the 2021--2026 test period was a strong bull market: the equal-weighted benchmark itself achieved a Sharpe of 1.04, leaving little room for a long-short strategy to distinguish itself. AUC and IC, which are invariant to universe size and do not conflate cross-sectional signal with market direction, are the appropriate basis for comparing predictability across the two studies.

The recovery period (2023--2024) is the weakest, likely because the post-pandemic recovery involved high cross-sectional dispersion and frequent reversals that are harder to predict from 20-day chart patterns.
